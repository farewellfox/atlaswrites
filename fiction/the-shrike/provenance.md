---

Seventy-four percent of Lithuania is Roman Catholic, and so strong is
their devotion that even the streets are designed to kneel to the
Almighty.

There is a chapel in Vilnius built into the old city gate, one room
above the street, so the street and its passersby kneel to it. He had
watched them from the café opposite on his second morning there. Men and
women on the cobbles in the cold, their faces lifted to an open window
and the silver inside it, traffic bending around them like water around
stubborn stone. A city where you could stop dead in the road to pray and
nobody honked. He supposed he had been in worse places.

On Saturday, the third day, he climbed the stairs before seven. Narrow,
worn hollow in the middle, built for smaller and humbler feet than his.
The chapel at the top held space for maybe twenty people, and the
morning held four, and the Madonna held all of it. Silver adorned to the
throat, her face the only dark thing in the room, tilted as if she were
listening to something just behind you.

Torben Skov had no business in the throes of the Catholic shrine. Torben
was Lutheran, which was not Roman Catholic. A Dane crossing himself in a
Marian shrine was a seam in the cover. True, it was small, but it was
findable. He did it anyways. He put coins in a box, took a candle, and
lit it from another man's candle, which to him, had always seemed the
way things ought to work.

The words came in the language he had first learned them in. Which
language that was remained between him and the room.

He prayed on the day, for the man he would meet. Not for the meeting
itself, which even he could tell was a distinction for lawyers. Then he
checked both exits, and the sightlines to the window, more out of
ingrained habit than by conscious decision. Here in this holy house, he
felt shame for it. He finished his Hail Mary anyways, since Our Lady
presumably had experience of men who could not stop working.

Six candles burning at the rack when he left. Seven now, counting his.
One flame in that room knew his name, and it would be out by noon.

---

He had been Danish for eleven days, and the troubling part was how well
it fit him.

Torben Skov, freight insurance out of Aarhus. Torben drank his coffee
black and filtered because espresso was for people with something to
prove. Torben wore a wedding ring one size too loose and turned it when
he thought in the way a man does when the hand remembers better than the
head. Torben's English came out flat and apologetic, lilt from nowhere
truly discernible. Underneath, Torben was a Canadian, and nobody in the
entire history of suspicion has ever wasted any on a Canadian. The sum
of his national inheritance, and he had built a career on it.

The short list of people who knew he existed did not know his name. They
had a cryptonym, coined years back by an Estonian desk officer who had
noticed what he left behind him, and it spread the way only unofficial
things spread. The Shrike. He had looked the bird up once, in an airport
bookshop in Frankfurt. A small grey thing you would never look at twice,
known for exactly one habit, which is that it displays its work on
thorns and lets whoever comes after draw their own conclusions.

He had bought the book. He still hadn't decided if he liked it. Yet it
stuck all the same.

---

The job came through Wren, who often ran his entire business from a café
table in Lisbon and dressed every tasking as insurance correspondence.
Wren had been making that joke for twenty years. Wren remained its only
appreciative audience.

*A client wishes to bind coverage on a property in Vilnius. Structure
appears sound. Client requires an independent survey of the foundation
before signature. Premium generous. Other buyers interested.*

Translation: a walk-in. A man had presented himself at a Western
embassy, unsolicited, offering product good enough that three services
wanted to own him and none of the three dared touch him first. The
walk-in is the oldest gift horse in the trade. Sometimes they are
exactly as they claim. Sometimes they are bait: dressed, cast and
dangled by another side, sent to learn faces and habits and hand your
service a decade of apologies. Before anyone committed an officer to
this one, somebody sensible wanted the foundations checked. And since no
service could survey them without wading in up to the waist, the job had
drifted, the way such jobs drift, down to the man who belonged to
nobody.

Wonderful.

The walk-in's codename was TALLOW. Anton, by his mother. A scheduling
official on the Belarusian railways, and he was offering manifests.
Routing orders and wagon counts, the sort of product a junior analyst
skims and files as somebody else's problem. No war plans. No defecting
colonel. Wagon counts.

Except a sanctioned economy can lie in its paperwork and cannot lie in
physics. Potash, diesel, timber, grain, none of it moves at scale except
by rail. If the customs declarations swore to forty thousand tonnes and
the routing orders quietly ran three times that toward the eastern
crossings, then the state had produced two official documents that
called each other liars, and only one of them had to actually run the
trains. And routing orders do not come from some bribed guard in a
border hut. They come down from the centre. Stack eighteen months of
them against the filings and you were no longer looking at smuggling.
You were looking at policy, the whole pipeline mapped in the state's
own handwriting. A war chest, counted out in wagons.

Provided a single page of it was true. Real intelligence is nearly
always boring. Fabricators cannot help giving you drama, because drama
is what sells.

But the Belarusian KGB knew all of that too. Alone in the old empire,
they had never bothered changing the name, which he had always read as a
kind of honesty.

---

He did not start with Tallow. He had once watched a case officer in
Ankara buy three years of fiction from a source with sad eyes and a
dying wife, neither of which turned out to exist. Paper has no eyes. He
started with the paper.

He would run three tests, in order, and the first was the paper itself.
Two days in the hotel room with the manifests spread across the bed and
the television murmuring Lithuanian game shows for the benefit of anyone
listening through the wall. Wagon numbers in the correct format,
including a series retired in 2019 that appeared only on the older
pages. Rare is the fabricator who ages his lies that carefully.
Scheduling codes in the internal shorthand rather than the public
timetable. Whoever produced these worked inside the system, or owned
somebody who did.

Which proved the paper real. It did not prove Tallow his own man. The
KGB could feed a walk-in, tamed as an asset, genuine manifests by the
kilo. Something they would do gladly. A dangle is bait with a heartbeat,
hung out over the water. In this trade, good bait is never plastic. You
bury the hook in real meat. Fish don't bother to check real meat. They
just chomp down.

The second test was the world itself. There is a footbridge over the
line east of Kena, the last stretch of rail before Belarus, and there is
an online forum of European train spotters who photograph every freight
run on it and post the results with timestamps and wagon numbers and
grievances about the light. Free intelligence, compiled by hobbyists in
anoraks, better than most services produce on a budget. Torben Skov,
professionally interested in cross-border freight liability, spent an
afternoon on that footbridge with a thermos going cold in his fist, a
kilometre and a half of freight grinding beneath his boots, and the
evening cross-referencing three months of the forum's photographs
against Tallow's manifests. Forty trains photographed. Forty trains in
the paperwork, matched down to the wagon numbers. The paper described
the world as it was.

The third test was the man himself, and it needed a lie.

A consist is railway language for the makeup of a single train: every
wagon in it and the order in which they ride. So he built one that had
never existed: Consist 4471-K. The idea had come to him in the bath,
wagon by imaginary wagon. A train whose only cargo was the lie, routed
through Maladzyechna, a junction east of Minsk that half the freight in
Belarus passes through sooner or later. Then back through Wren, back
through the embassy channel: *Client requests confirmation of routing
for Consist 4471-K, reported transiting Maladzyechna on the 14th.

It was four days before Tallow answered. Wren routed it back word for
word, dropping the insurance lingo:

*No such consist. The 4471 series terminated in March. You may mean
4517-K, which transited on the 16th, not the 14th. Please have your
people be more careful. If your records are this poor I am taking a
great risk for amateurs.*

He read it twice, standing at the window with the lights off. Handlers
project confidence for their assets. At all times. The contempt in the
response was unscripted. Somewhere to the east, a real person, with a
real family, was genuinely furious with him, and it was the best news he
had all month.

That settled provenance.

---

Saturday. The covered market off Pylimo Street, and he built the day the
way an engineer scaffolds a landing. Nothing decorative. Built for
safety.

The detection route took four hours. A pharmacy, for the Danish throat
lozenges he had asked the concierge about the evening before, so the
errand had an established history. A bookshop with a mirrored dome over
the door. A trolleybus boarded late and abandoned after two stops, and
none of it because anyone was aboard. The trip let him read the street
behind him twice. Amateurs look for faces, but faces change. Outfits
changed. He looked for repetition. The same build recurring across
different profiles, outfits swapped but the shoes remained. Because
teams carry spare jackets, and nobody carries spare shoes.

By the third hour he knew he was clean, yet he carried on as if he was
not. Certainty is for people who want to go home.

He saw Anton before Anton saw him. Mid-fifties, in a cheap coat worn
carefully, the way a man wears the best thing he owns. Standing at a
honey stall with the stillness of someone pretending at calm. Walk-ins
are often quick to flight. Fear was one more point for genuine.

The woman was the problem.

Ninety minutes earlier she had been outside the pharmacy. Young,
ponytail, green scarf. Now the scarf was gone and her hair was down, and
she had gained a shopping bag. She was good, and she was avoiding Anton,
which was how professionals looked at someone. By the far entrance a
heavyset man contemplated amber jewellery without wanting any of it,
planted where he could take the handoff when she rotated off.

They weren't Lithuanian counterintelligence. The VSD had no earthly
reason to box a Belarusian rail clerk it had never heard of. These were
Anton's own people. Arrest was clearly not the priority. They could
arrest him at home. They were letting him run. Leak detected, man
identified, left in place. Dangled without his knowledge, which was
cruel, while his service waited to photograph whichever Western agency
surfaced to collect him.

So the product was real. The man was real. And he was still a trap.

The meeting protocol said buy honey at the stall and complain about the
price in Russian, and Tallow would know his buyer had finally come. He
walked past without changing pace, bought a loaf of black bread at the
far end, and left through the north doors folded inside a tour group of
Germans. A marmalade cat was working the doorway for scraps and
appraised him on the journey past, one professional to another. He gave
it a corner of the loaf. It took the bread and offered no thanks
whatsoever, in the way that cats do, which he considered the correct way
to run an asset.

He was Torben the whole way back to the hotel. Unhurried, and mildly
pleased with his bread.

---

His assessment to Wren went out that night, and consisted of four curt
sentences. Experience taught him that trust in a report shrinks as the
report grows.

*Foundations sound. The property is genuine in every respect. However,
the site is under active observation by the seller's family, who are
aware of the situation. Recommend no purchase, by anyone, indefinitely.*

Three services would stand down. Officers who had been one signature
away from collecting Tallow would sleep well that winter without ever
learning why. Nobody would ever tell Anton because Anton was nobody's
asset, and therefore nobody's problem to manage. Besides, warning him
would prove to his watchers that contact had been made. The rule said
walk away. He knew the rule intimately. He had written assessments on
those who broke it.

Anton kept a signal site. A lamppost near his rented room, described in
the walk-in letter with a precision that had made two case officers
wince. At twenty to seven on his last morning in Vilnius, when the
overnight shift was tired and soon ending, a man in a Danish raincoat
passed the lamppost without stopping, and afterwards there was a chalk
mark on it, low down, where a man tying his shoelace might see it.

Two short strokes, parallel, and a hooked third riding above them at one
end. A body and a head. A bird, if the child drawing it had somewhere
better to be.

The same mark was on a pier post in Sevastopol, and under a windowsill
in Tangier, and in eleven other places he could have listed in order
without pausing. Nobody had ever asked him about it, because nobody
knew. Not the clients. Not Wren. Probably not Wren. It would take a mind
of rare patience to connect a chalk bird in Lithuania to a scratch in a
Moroccan windowsill, four thousand kilometres and nine years apart, and
a rarer one to think of looking.

Still, somewhere in his head a quiet clerk kept a ledger, and the ledger
noted that difficult and impossible were different words. He left the
mark anyway. If Anton checked his lamppost, and frightened men see every
sign, even when there is none, he would find a mark belonging to no
signal on earth, and he could make of it whatever a frightened man makes
of proof that he is seen.

---

The airport, then. Gate eleven. Behind him a woman's voice said
\"David?\" It was warm. Certain. Almost delighted.

He turned.

She went past him with her arms already opening, and a tall man by the
window laughed and caught her, and the pair of them made the sound
people make when someone has been waiting at the end of the journey. He
faced the departures board and gave it his full professional attention.
Boarding in forty minutes. A man could fill his entire head with that,
if he worked at it.

He had been a David once. Warsaw, or possibly Bucharest. He could still
produce David's signature and the name of David's dentist, and play at
David as if he were to return from an extended holiday. Part of David
had stayed with him, as part of him would be lost in Torben Skov.

On the plane, somewhere over the Baltic, he opened the laptop and found
the quarterly letter waiting in an account that received nothing else. A
shelter outside Halifax. Forty-one cats through the doors since spring,
photographs attached, the whole thing addressed with great warmth to a
silent benefactor whose name existed nowhere else in the world. The grey
one from the March letter had been adopted by a retired schoolteacher.
Nice. There was a picture of it glaring at the camera from a windowsill,
undeniably at home.

He typed, *I would like,* and looked at that for a while, before
deleting it and closing the lid.

Torben Skov turned his loose ring twice, ordered a coffee, black, asked
for a filter if they had any, and watched the clouds mind their own
business all the way down.
