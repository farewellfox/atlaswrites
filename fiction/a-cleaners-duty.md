## Part 1

### 1

The train ran north the way a debt collector knocks. Steady, unhurried,
no doubt at all about being let in. Spauld sat near the centre of the
cabin with his arms crossed and his eyes half shut, letting the bench
creak into his back with every turn of the wheels. Familiar noise. Today
was a day like any other, and in his line of work you wanted as many of
those as you could collect.

Across from him, Petey Blanick had his face pressed to the window,
wide-eyed at grey hills crawling past like he'd never seen dirt before.
Wiry, freckled, reddish-blond hair poking out from under a helmet two
sizes too big. The kid still had that eager fire the world hadn't
pissed on yet. Spauld gave it a month. He remembered his own first ride
well enough. His breakfast had painted the inside of his visor before
they even cleared Northrail, and the man across from him hadn't said a
word about it, which had been its own kind of mercy.

Beside Petey slouched his uncle. Gord Blanick, bald, broad, a week of
stubble, snoring softly with his chin buried in his chest plate. The
incinerator pack leaned against his knee like an old dog. Gord slept
anywhere he was allowed to, because he knew exactly how little of it
he'd get once his boots swapped metal for dirt. Spauld respected that.
Rest was a supply like any other. You drew it when the drawing was good.

Moll ran another check on her medkit, dark braid pulled tight, eyes
narrowed in a way that had nothing to do with the straps. The straps
were fine. The straps were always fine. But routine gave fear less room
to breathe, and Moll had been doing this long enough to know that fear
didn't need much.

Petey peeled his face off the glass. \"Moll. You done many Garrote
runs?\"

\"Two.\" She didn't look up from the kit. \"Three if you count the one
we turned around on.\"

\"Why'd you turn around?\"

\"Bridge was gone. Sprawl took the pilings in the night, grew up through
forty feet of iron like it was potting soil.\" She cinched a strap.
\"Rules say you don't burn load-bearing growth till engineering signs
off. So we logged it, flagged it, went home. A month on, some other
squad burned it anyway and dropped the whole span in the river.\"

\"They catch trouble for it?\"

\"Commendation.\" She snapped the kit shut. \"That's the job, Petey.
Nine parts procedure, one part fire, and you'll spend a career learning
which part they'll thank you for.\"

\"Uncle says the fire's the whole point.\"

\"Your uncle carries the fire. He would say that.\"

\"Fire's honest,\" Gord rumbled, eyes still shut. \"Paper ain't.\"

Spauld listened without seeming to. Contain, burn, log. The whole trade
in three verbs, and you learned a lot about a Cleaner by which one they
put first. Gord burned. Moll logged. Earl contained, everything, always.
Spauld had never settled on his own order, which was probably why they
used him for the gaps.

Spauld let his head rest against the cool wall and did his own version
of the same thing, which was to sit still and count the squad's weight.
One green, one old, one steady, one in charge. And him. Whatever he was.
Filler, mostly. The man you slotted in where the gap was.

Somewhere up the line, Fort Ironmarch waited. Beyond that, the Garrote.

---

Northrail came and went without ceremony. Signs of life slid past the
window. A refuelling platform mid-shift, cranes paused, two figures with
their hands deep in patched coats, watching the train the way you watch
weather.

No one in the cabin stirred. Petey had finally nodded off with his
fingers still looped through his harness. Moll sat with her arms crossed
and her eyes shut, breathing too evenly to be asleep. Gord hadn't moved
in an hour.

Spauld watched the two figures on the platform until the fog took them.
Nobody waved at a Cleaner train. Nobody ever had, in his memory, and
he'd stopped deciding whether that was respect or the other thing.

---

The train hissed and shuddered to a stop. No whistle. Just the long
groan of metal giving up its momentum.

\"Gear and pack check.\" Knuckles rapped twice on the cabin door.
\"Thirty seconds. Up and at 'em.\"

Erlan Morden stood in the threshold. Earl to the squad, sir to his face,
salt-and-pepper buzz and a face full of lines that each looked
individually earned. He didn't raise his voice. Didn't need to.

He scanned them in silence. Moll was already up, cinching her thigh
pouch. Petey blinked awake and fumbled his harness. Gord stirred,
muttered something unkind to the morning, and hauled the incinerator
pack onto his knee.

Spauld rose last, ran his fingers over the latches on his chest rig,
thumbed the release on his rifle strap, gave it all one practised tug.
Tight. Good.

Earl's eyes stayed on him a second longer than the others. They always
did. Spauld had never asked why and Earl had never offered, and between
them that passed for a friendship.

### 2

They stepped down into wind with an inland bitterness to it, the kind
that skipped the flesh and went straight for the bone. Spauld stretched
his legs and read the fort while the others sorted their straps.

Fort Ironmarch wore abandonment like a shoddy umbrella you overpaid for.
The walls stood, but they leaned in places, and lean was a habit walls
learned from the men on them. A pair of guards drifted over with rifles
slung low and postures that said the most dangerous thing they'd fought
this year was the duty roster. This was a dead-end post. The money went
to the Divide. What was left came here, and what was left of that had
clearly gone for a pack of smokes and not bothered coming home.

Behind him, Petey leaned in close to his uncle's rig and frowned.

\"Uncle. Your regulator's venting ether. Pressure valve might be
sticky.\"

Gord grunted. \"Just the wind.\"

\"Nah. Wind don't smell sweet. That's vent bleed. Not enough to blow,
but enough to burn if we spark wrong.\" The kid hesitated, then added,
quieter, \"'Specially in this wind.\"

Gord waved him off. Spauld noted it. True, the kid talked too much, but
he'd noticed something a fort's whole maintenance crew apparently
hadn't.

A guard ambled up while they sorted themselves, an older man with a
crank handle over his shoulder and the unhurried walk of somebody whose
war was mostly weather. He looked the squad over, gear, faces, the
incinerator pack, and his eyes did a small arithmetic Spauld had seen
before on ferrymen and gravediggers.

\"Headed up to the outpost, then.\"

\"Status run,\" Earl said.

\"Ayuh.\" The guard glanced north at the hills, then back. \"Patrol went
up in the spring. Log says they rotated south after.\" He rolled
something around his mouth and spat. \"Funny thing. Nobody here watched
'em come back through. And there's the one road.\"

\"Anything worth saying, say it plain,\" Earl said.

The guard shrugged, already turning for the gatehouse. \"Just sayin'
the log's a comfort. Long as you don't stand too close to it.\"

Earl didn't answer. Didn't note it, didn't take the man's name,
didn't do any of the things a squad leader does with word of a missing
patrol. Spauld watched him not do them, and added it to the stack that
was becoming a cairn.

They passed through the gate, which opened slowly and hitched once, like
it had forgotten and then remembered how. Inside was a dreary concoction
of concrete and prefab, rust and old ash. Two comms towers, one blinking
red, one long dead. And near the inner track, a stretch of soil that
stopped Spauld mid-stride.

Too clean. No ash. No shoots. No frost-heave, even. Just bare earth in a
neat blank patch, like something had passed through and taken the roots
out with it. He crouched, pressed two fingers into the dirt. Loose on
top. Packed underneath.

Burned ground told a story. Cut ground told a story. This told nothing,
and nothing was a story too, if somebody had gone to the trouble of
writing it.

\"Welcome to paradise,\" Moll muttered beside him, pulling her gloves
on.

Petey squinted up at the dead tower and spat. \"This it? Don't look
like much.\"

\"It ain't, nephew.\" Gord's voice came out flat. \"Neglect'll gut a
place quicker than the Sprawl.\"

Above the command prefab door, someone had spray-painted over the
official sign in red stencilled letters. THE BLEEDIN' NORTH. Spauld
looked at it, then at the guards who walked past it every day and had
decided it could stay.

Last outpost before the Garrote, and his was the only squad going
through.

---

Inside Command, dust hung in the light and a terminal buzzed under a
stack of unread reports, their headers flashing red at no one. The man
at the desk waved them through without looking up.

\"Status update only,\" Earl said.

Moll tapped in the squad code. Static flared. She adjusted the dial and
spoke with clipped authority.

\"Squad Iron-Three reporting. Arrived Ironmarch. Proceeding north.\"

Dead air hissed back at her.

\"Confirmation, Moll?\"

She waited another second, then turned. \"Signal bounced. Relay took it.
Should be logged.\"

Should be. Spauld watched Earl take that in. A relay log meant nobody at
the other end had to be listening, and nobody at the other end had to
answer, and there'd be a record saying everything worked as intended.
Earl nodded once and turned for the door. He didn't ask when the last
squad's status had come through this panel. It was the obvious
question. It was the only question, standing where they were standing.

Earl didn't ask it. Spauld put that next to the too-clean dirt and kept
walking.

### 3

At the north gate, Gord adjusted the incinerator pack with a grunt and
Petey fell in half a step behind him, eyes on the craggy hills.

\"Uncle. You think the Sprawl's already in the Garrote?\"

\"Sprawl's everywhere, boy. Whether it's showin' or not. Might just
be seedin'. Might be watchin'.\"

\"What's it like?\"

Gord slowed. When he spoke again the drawl was the same, but something
had gone out from under it. \"Ain't like anything. It don't scream,
don't roar, don't chase you with claws. It's patient. Grows under
your boots through the ground you step. Once it's in, you won't feel
it. You'll step like normal, right up until you're turned on us.\"

He let that sit.

\"Stay tight. Mask sealed if ordered. Out here, if your gut says
somethin' ain't right, it likely ain't, and you speak up. Don't act
tough for nobody. That's how you end up a story someone else tells.\"

Petey nodded, quiet for once. Spauld caught the edge in the old man's
voice and knew it for what it was. That hadn't been a warning. That had
been a memory with the names filed off.

The quiet didn't hold. Petey was a man with more questions than
pockets, and he turned the next one on Moll, walking his other side.
\"You ever seen one? A blooming. Up close.\"

Moll checked the seal on a vial and took her time putting it away. \"I
was a medic in the belt before I was this. Circuit practice. Three
villages and a lot of road between them.\" The wind worried at her
braid. \"Fourth summer, a hand off the Tollery farm came in with what
looked like a splinter gone bad. Whole forearm hot. Lines running up
past the elbow.\"

\"Weren't a splinter,\" Petey said.

\"No.\" She let the wind have the silence a moment. \"I did what the
Council pamphlet says. Reported it, kept him comfortable, kept everyone
else out of the room. Pamphlet says a squad comes inside three days.\"
She adjusted the strap of her medkit, which didn't need adjusting.
\"Took nine.\"

Petey didn't ask what the six days cost.

\"That why you joined?\" Petey asked instead.

\"I joined so nobody else waits nine days. Pamphlet says three. Ought to
be some truth to that.\" She said it flat. \"I trust the pamphlet's
arithmetic about as far as I can spit. But I'd rather be the part of
the machine that shows up.\"

Spauld checked his own mask seal and said nothing. Every Cleaner he'd
ridden with carried some version of the same story, the day the job
stopped being a pamphlet. He'd never heard Moll's before. Nine days.
He filed it with the rest.

## Part 2

### 1

The gate to the north opened with a slower reluctance than the welcome.
Beyond it the terrain narrowed into a jagged channel between
weather-scoured slopes, a few metres wide in the pinch points. The
Garrote. Whoever named it had been in it.

birthThe wind found them immediately. It didn't blow through the
channel so much as get sharpened by it. Every breath went down like
something borrowed.

\"Single file,\" Earl said. \"Eyes wide.\"

Spauld took second, tracing the slopes for ambush lines out of habit.
Behind him the squad strung out in silence, boots on frost-cracked
stone, and the wind did all the talking for a hundred metres.

Then Petey's voice cracked it. \"Uncle! Down, drop the pack, DOWN!\"

Spauld spun in time to see it. A tongue of blue flame licking out of
Gord's regulator housing, thin and almost pretty, bent flat by the wind
and feeling along the backplate like a hand looking for a door. Vented
ether, sparked off stone or static or nothing at all. Gord was already
twisting, reaching back for straps his arms were twenty years too thick
to find.

Petey got there first. The kid didn't grab his uncle. He grabbed the
rig. Flipped the bleed valve, wrenched the feed line a quarter turn, and
slapped the emergency dump with the heel of his hand, and the flame
guttered from starved to gone in the space of a breath. The stink of
scorched ceramic rolled over them. Gord stood very still, one glove
smoking gently.

\"Off,\" Petey said, and his voice only shook a little. \"Let me see the
valve.\"

Gord shrugged the pack down. His forearm above the glove had gone an
angry red where the backplate had cooked through the sleeve. He looked
at it the way a man looks at rain.

\"Sticky valve,\" Petey muttered, already into the housing with two
fingers and a probe. \"Told you it was bleedin'. Seat's worn. I can
shim it, but you're carryin' a temper on your back till we're home.\"
He looked up at his uncle and his face did something complicated. \"You
gotta listen to me when it's the gear. I ain't smart about much. I'm
smart about this.\"

\"Ayuh,\" Gord said. Just that. But he sat down on a rock to let Moll
dress the arm, and he watched the kid work the valve, and whatever
passed over his face wasn't something he'd have wanted words for
anyway.

Earl stood over them. \"Time?\"

\"Ten minutes,\" Petey said.

\"Take eight.\"

Spauld drifted the perimeter while they worked. Which is how he found
it. Twenty paces on, in the lee of a boulder, a patch of undergrowth
that had stopped being undergrowth. He crouched, ran a gloved hand
through loose dirt, and found the crust underneath. Brittle. Chemical
sheen. Virex-9 residue, weeks old, and concentrated in a tight circle
instead of arced across the ground the way a containment sweep threw it.

Moll crouched beside him, arm dressing done, and brushed a second patch
clear. Same thing. Blackened, compacted earth in a neat ring.

\"You don't get a controlled root kill like this with a sweep,\" she
said, low.

\"And you don't rebury it after.\" He rubbed the residue between finger
and thumb. \"Sweeps scar. Somebody burned the base of something and
lifted it out whole. Same clean lines as that patch in the fort.\"

She looked at him. \"An extraction.\"

Everything he'd been taught said the Sprawl got burned where it stood.
You killed it or it killed you, and either way it stayed dead where it
fell. Nobody harvested it. Nobody had a reason to. He'd worked with
zealots and mercs and bureaucrats, and the one thing all of them agreed
on, the single article of shared faith in the whole bleeding north, was
that the green went in the fire.

Someone had stopped agreeing.

\"Earl,\" he called. \"We're not the first ones through here.\"

Earl came, looked, and was quiet for exactly as long as a man is quiet
when he's deciding what to say. Not how to read the ground. What to say
about it.

\"Noted,\" he said. \"We move.\"

Half a second too fast. Spauld put that next to the question Earl
hadn't asked at the fort. The stack was getting heavy.

### 2

They found the real thing an hour deeper in, where the channel kinked
and a seep of groundwater came down the north face in a dark stain.
Growth stood in the wet crack, thorned and dense, a body's length of it
and climbing, dull green shading to a bruise colour at the tips. The ash
lantern at Spauld's belt woke as they came near, its glow swelling like
a stirred coal.

\"Pocket,\" Earl said. \"Small one. By the book.\"

Nobody had to be told twice, which is most of what a squad is. Moll read
the wind off a pinch of dust and posted herself upslope with the
sprayer, laying a wet line of dilute nine along the rock where the burn
would be allowed to stop. Petey got in behind his uncle's rig and into
the housing, muttering to it, pressure counts and valve grades, the shim
earning its keep. Spauld took the high ground with his rifle on the
channel ahead, because fire drew things in the borderlands, and
something always wanted to know what was cooking.

And Gord walked the growth.

That was the part nobody had ever explained to Spauld and nobody ever
did. Before any burn, every burn, the big man walked the length of the
green with his eyes down, slow, a farmer pricing a hedge. He toed the
base of it. Crouched once, moved a thorned runner aside with the flat of
his knife, looked at what was under it, and stood.

\"What's he lookin' for?\" Petey whispered from the housing.

\"Fuel load,\" Gord said, without turning around. \"Wet root burns
dirty.\"

Spauld watched the knife go back in its sheath. He had seen Gord check
fuel load in rain and in drought, in growth wet enough to squelch and
brittle enough to snap, and the check never changed and the answer never
seemed to matter. Whatever the man was looking for under there, he'd
been looking a long time, and Spauld hoped, in a general way, that he
never found it.

The growth had gone still. That was the thing about the green the
pamphlets didn't teach. It moved, always, just under the eye's
threshold, a slow bloodless restlessness, right up until you came near
with fire in your hands. Then it stopped. Every stem, every runner, the
whole pocket standing dead calm the way a man stands calm when he's
decided the sentry might not see him.

\"It knows,\" Petey said. Barely a breath.

\"It don't know anything.\" Gord settled the torch nozzle. \"Knowin's
for things that end. Light your lamp and stay upwind.\"

The burn went in low and blue and patient, and the pocket came apart the
way they always did. A hiss, a sweet green stink, a crackle rising to a
soft roar, and once, briefly, a sound underneath it all that Spauld had
heard on a dozen burns and had elected never to describe to anyone. Gord
ran the flame along the root line twice. Moll's wet perimeter held. The
wind took the smoke up the channel and away, the lantern banked from
coal to ember, and it was over inside ten minutes.

Contain, burn, log. The job, done right, in country where somebody else
had been doing something else entirely.

Spauld crouched at the scar afterward while Petey helped his uncle shed
the rig. Char, ash arcs, the ground scorched honest. He set it against
the tight buried circles behind them. This was what a kill looked like.
It left a mark and it left a smell and it lied to nobody. Whoever was
working these hills had learned to kill without leaving either, and
there was no version of that skill a man acquired for good reasons.

\"Log it,\" Earl said. \"One pocket, contained. Move.\"

Moll logged it. It occurred to Spauld, watching her write, that it was
the first entry of the whole trip that was purely true.

### 3

The Garrote gave out into a shallow basin ringed by cracked hills, and
the outpost sat squat against the far side of it. More prefab than
fortification. Two towers flanked the entrance, floodlights dark, and a
door somewhere was swinging on its hinges, creak and bang, creak and
bang, the loneliest sound Spauld knew of.

They paused at the ridge. No sentries. No smoke. No patrols. The
buildings stood in a crescent around a yard gone to brittle grass.
MEDICAL, OPS, STORAGE, stencils faded to ghosts. COMMAND at the centre
of the curve. The kind of post built fast, staffed light, and forgotten
the day a newer rail segment opened.

Earl raised a hand. \"Spauld, Moll. Inside sweep. Gord, perimeter with
the kid. Storage first. I want to know how long this place has been
cold.\"

They crossed the open ground and took the crescent in order. The MEDICAL
prefab stood unlocked, which said plenty on its own. Inside, the drug
locker hung open and stripped, but the rest had been left as it was
lived in. Two cots with the blankets thrown back. A razor and a cracked
mirror by the basin. A paper of boiled sweets gone cloudy with age. On
the small desk, a letter, half written, the pen laid across it as though
the writer meant to be back before the ink skinned over.

Spauld read the first lines because reading things was the job. \*Dear
Essa. The cold here is the talking kind, you'd hate it. Tell your
mother the pay comes through the Corindry office now, ask for.\* There
it stopped, mid-name, mid-errand, the most ordinary sentence never
finished in the north.

He laid it back the way he'd found it. Moll watched him do it and said
nothing, and neither of them suggested taking it, because taking it
meant deciding what it was evidence of.

OPS was barer. A duty roster chalked on slate, names in a column, the
last date against them weeks old. Somebody had begun wiping the board
and stopped a third of the way through, and the smeared arc of the rag
hung there mid-stroke, same as everything else in this place. Started,
interrupted, left.

\"That date's from before our tasking was even cut,\" Moll said.

\"It's from before a lot of things.\" Spauld looked at the half-erased
names and didn't read them aloud. There was an inventory forming in his
head, cots and letters and boards, and every item on it said the same
thing. These people had left inside ten minutes, expecting to come back,
and hadn't.

Command last. Spauld nudged its door open with his rifle muzzle and met
no resistance, which was its own kind of answer.

Inside, shadow and thin blades of light through gapped seams. Dry air.
One corridor, two rooms. In comms, cables hung from the ceiling like
stripped nerves. Moll checked the lockers. Empty. Spauld tapped the
central terminal. Dead. Power cord severed, battery housing pilfered.

Moll crouched at the supply rack and brushed dust off twisted anchor
bolts. \"They took the mounts. Stripped it to the frame.\"

\"That's not scavengers.\" He turned slowly, reading the room.
Scavengers ripped. This was disassembly. \"Somebody showed them where
the bolts were.\"

Earl's boots in the doorway, then Gord ducking through the frame, Petey
behind.

\"Report.\"

\"No hostiles, no bodies, no gear,\" Moll said. \"Picked clean.\"

\"Clean's the word,\" Spauld said. \"No fight. Somebody scrubbed
through with purpose.\"

Earl gave a single nod, jaw tight, and turned to go. Spauld didn't
follow. The emptiness itched at him, and he'd learned a long time ago
that the itch was cheaper to scratch than to ignore. He went back to the
centre console and ran his hand along the underside where crews stashed
backup logs. The latch popped with a dry click.

A folded sheet of paper dropped to the floor.

He picked it up and read it alone, back to the door. Rushed hand,
standard Cleaner shorthand, a few lines only.

\*Sprawl signs beyond the ridge. Growth patterns unnatural. Roots
clean-cut. Reported twice. No acknowledgement.\*

Reported twice. He read it a third time anyway.

A note hidden under a console meant one of two things. Orders ignored,
or truths silenced. And the man who'd written it hadn't known which
either, or he'd have sent it up the line instead of leaving it here in
the dark for whoever came next. For me, Spauld thought, without much
liking it.

His first instinct was to walk it straight to Earl, because that was the
drill and the drill existed for good reasons. His second instinct was
the one he trusted. Whoever this note was hidden from had worn the same
coat he did. Until he knew which links in the chain were rusted, handing
it up the chain was just burying it a second time.

He folded it into the inner sleeve of his coat. He'd tell the squad.
The squad he could see. It was the parts of the Cleaners he couldn't
see that the paper needed protecting from, and if that was the wrong
call, well. He'd made wrong calls with worse consequences and slept
fine. Mostly fine.

### 4

Outside, the wind dragged dust across the yard in listless spirals.
Petey stood up from a busted fuel drum as Spauld came out.

\"We checked stock. Rations full, water drums full, spare filters
stacked and sealed.\" He wiped his glove across his nose. \"Who packs up
and leaves that behind?\"

\"Folks in a hurry ain't worried 'bout rations,\" Gord said. \"Dead
don't eat.\"

Spauld took the note out and read it to them in the middle of the yard,
plain, no preamble. Then he told them where he'd found it and let the
silence do its work.

\"Reported twice,\" Moll repeated. Her voice had gone very level, which
anyone who knew her understood was the opposite of calm. \"Our orders
came from the SL. We were told nothing about prior contact.\"

\"Then either the reports died between here and Ashbarrow,\" Spauld
said, \"or they arrived fine.\"

Petey looked between them, working it through, getting there. \"Wait.
Are you sayin' Earl knew?\"

Nobody answered. And then, without anyone meaning it, they were all
looking at Spauld. He felt the weight of the look and wanted to hand it
back. He hadn't asked to be the man people looked at. He'd spent a
career being the man people looked past, and it suited him, and some
corner of him noted with dry disgust that the one sure way to get made
leader of anything was to visibly not want the job.

\"If he knew,\" Spauld said, \"he had reasons for not saying.\"

\"And if he didn't,\" Moll said, softer, \"someone wanted it that
way.\"

Which was worse. None of them said so. All of them thought it.

---

They made camp in the yard. No fire. Ration packs, muted voices, the
clink of gear being stripped, cleaned, reassembled by hands that needed
something to do.

Spauld sat on a low concrete block with his back to the wind, sorting
ammo vials, holding each to the last of the light. Checking for cracks,
faded glow, chemical separation. Chemics was half the trade. Nothing
worse than scorch gel deciding to leak on your lap.

Across the yard, Moll drifted over to Petey with her medkit slung low
and a metal flask in one hand. Too far for words, so Spauld read the
shapes instead. Her crouch, easy and unhurried, the crouch of a woman
approaching a skittish field mouse. The flask held out. The kid's
sniff, the flinch, the sip, the cough that bent him double while Moll's
shoulders shook once with something she didn't let become a laugh. Then
talk. He couldn't hear it and didn't need to. He watched the kid's
hands, which had been picking and picking at his boot heel, go gradually
still. Watched the shoulders come down from around his ears. Watched
Moll put a palm on his shoulder before she stood, and say one last thing
that made the kid sit up an inch taller.

She had a gift. Spauld could keep a squad alive with a rifle and Moll
could do it with a flask of paint-stripper and ten minutes, and
privately he suspected hers was the more durable method.

She passed him on her way back and didn't stop. \"He'll do.\"

\"Never doubted.\"

\"Liar,\" she said, comfortably, and moved on.

Gord came and sat on the block beside him a while later, easing down
with the sigh of a man whose knees invoiced him separately. He said
nothing for a time, which from Gord was conversation. He watched the
dark past the wall, and Spauld sorted vials, and the wind moved the
brittle grass.

\"That ground today,\" Gord said at last. \"The circles.\"

Spauld waited.

\"Seen ground done like that before. Once.\" The old man turned a ration
tin slowly in his hands without opening it. \"South of here. Long time
back.\"

\"Where south?\"

The tin went still. For a moment something moved behind Gord's face, a
door tried from the inside, and Spauld watched him decide, and watched
the decision go the way it had clearly gone for twenty years.

\"Don't matter. Wasn't the same.\" Gord stood, knees filing their
objection, and picked up his rifle. \"I'll take the wall till second
watch. Kid sleeps through, you hear? Whatever hour it turns
interestin', it can be interestin' without him.\"

He walked to the wall. Spauld looked after him and filed it, south of
here, long time back, next to the fuel-load checks and the flat place in
the man's voice when he'd talked about the green turning people. There
was a shape under all of it. Spauld could feel the edges. He was in no
hurry to see the whole of it, and he suspected that when he did, it
wouldn't be because Gord chose to show him.

Earl's voice cut across the yard. \"Twenty minutes. Then we move.\"

### 5

They moved north in staggered file through thin forest, boots crunching
moss and brittle rootbed. The deeper they went, the stranger the ground
got. The soil turned chalky. No erosion channels, no pooling.

\"No,\" Spauld said. Fear was for things that were on their own.

Earl watched the ridge a long moment. \"Spauld, Moll, follow its line.

Spauld turned with his sidearm level. The man in the doorway didn't
move and didn't seem to mind. Middle years, soft grey coat cut for a
city, hands loose at his sides and visibly empty. On his collar, small
enough to miss if you weren't hungry, a wheat sheaf done in gold.

\"He asked us for that,\" the man said. \"Kind of you to oblige. They
felt it go easier than most, you know. The others. He kept telling them
you'd given your word. Bare-faced, no less.\" A small tilt of the head.
\"That's why I walked down to this end, if you're wondering. The
crates load themselves. A Cleaner who unseals to keep a promise to a
dying stranger is worth the walk.\"

\"You're told all this.\"

\"I don't wear the green myself.\" A small smile. \"Someone has to stay
legible.\"

Spauld read him the way he read a lock he hadn't been given time for.
The coat said city. The voice said nowhere, a register built to belong
to whichever room it stood in. The hands stayed loose and open and
exactly one stride beyond reaching distance, which was a professional's
courtesy, offered the way professionals offer things, so you'd know it
could be withdrawn. Behind him, down the corridor, the dolly wheel
squeaked its rounds. The crates were still moving. Nothing about this
conversation was slowing the work.

He came one step into the room, unhurried, and looked Spauld over like a
clerk checking goods against a bill. \"Iron-Three. Morden commanding.
Mollock, comms and medical. The Blanicks, elder and younger, on the
burner. Which leaves you. Thomas Spaulder. Scout, infiltration,
fill-in.\" The recitation was flat, a man reading his own paperwork
back. \"You were meant to find cold ash and an empty basin, you know.
Jerrek ran, and farmers were ever a difficult crop. It put us behind and
you ahead, and here we both are.\"

Spauld kept the sidearm where it was and put his back near the terminal.
The core ticked through its copy behind him, and he needed another
minute of this man's voice, so he went and bought it with a name.

\"Halvery signs your requisitions.\"

The man's face did nothing at all, and did it a half-second too
carefully.

\"That's not a name I'd say aloud down here,\" he said.

\"No. Your clerks said it for you.\"

\"Mm.\" The man let it go the way you let go of a coin already spent.
\"But since we're being familiar. Consider. You've walked the belt.
You've seen what the Council leaves out there. They salt a valley's
water rights to feed a rail subsidy and call it policy, and when the
valley starves quietly, that's order, and when it objects, that's
insurrection.\"

Spauld had walked the belt. He'd drunk from wells with a Council meter
bolted to the winch, eaten at tables where the children were counted
against the ration schedule before they were counted for anything else.
None of it needed confirming out loud, and the man watched him not
confirm it, and looked satisfied anyway, which is what listening for a
living got you.

\"The Divide is losing, Cleaner,\" the man went on. \"You know it. Nine
burns hotter every year and holds less every year, and the day it fails,
the only people still standing in the north will be the ones the green
has agreed to keep. I am making sure some of those people are ours.\"

\"Ours.\"

\"The belt's. The forgotten's. Rural independence, if you want the
pamphlet word.\"

And there it was, laid out neat as the man's collar, and Spauld felt
the shape of it settle. Because he had walked the belt. He'd seen the
salted valleys. Every word of the grievance was true, which is what made
it such excellent bait.

\"Independence,\" Spauld said. \"Grown in your tanks. On your titration.
Dreaming on a network your people built and your people can read.\" He
nodded slowly. \"Jerrek thought he was an uprising. He was a crop.
You're the glove on this, and Halvery's the hand inside it, and when
the Divide fails, your free belt will belong to him down to the last
Sprawl-walking soul of it, bred loyal, bought hungry. He'll be the one
man in Ashbarrow who owns people the green can't touch.\"

The smile didn't change, which told Spauld more than a flinch would
have. \"Even if you're right,\" the man said. \"Even if it is control.
Whose control would you rather starve under, the Council that salts the
water, or a man who at least needs the belt alive?\" He spread his empty
hands. \"You could matter too, you know. You've the look of a man
who's tired of being spent by people who don't count the change.\"

\"Meaning what. For form's sake.\"

\"Meaning that when the Divide fails, there will be lists. Who stands
inside the new ground, and who stands outside it. Somebody holds the pen
early, and early is everything with lists.\" He said it the way other
men discussed seating at a wedding. \"A person who walks into a strange
archive and comes out with the one name that matters would be worth a
line high up the page. His squad too, if that's the shape of his
sentiment. I make no bones about buying sentiment. It's the only
durable loyalty on the market.\"

And for one count, one, Spauld stood in it. Filler, the file said, and
the file was good, and here at last was somebody offering to count the
change. He gave the feeling its count, because pretending it wasn't
there would only leave it loose behind him, and then he set it down
where he set such things down.

He talked like a friend. Jerrek's phrase, and standing in front of the
whole of it, Spauld finally understood the horror. The man didn't lie.
He didn't have to.

\"Here's how the next ten minutes go,\" Spauld said. \"Your crew
finishes loading and leaves by whatever hole it came in. You go with
them. In eleven minutes this plant has an ether fault, and everything
left down here burns with it.\"

\"You do know the fault does half my work for me,\" the man said. \"We
came to erase this place. You're volunteering for the shovel.\"

\"I know. Fire's cheap.\" Spauld tapped two fingers against his coat,
over the core and the note. \"What fire can't hand back to Halvery
already left the room.\"

The man studied him. \"And me. You're letting me walk.\"

\"I'm letting the glove walk.\" Spauld stepped forward, and with the
sidearm still level, reached out and plucked the wheat-sheaf pin from
the grey collar. The man permitted it, curious. \"Shooting you tells the
hand exactly how much we know and exactly which squad to bury. Letting
you walk means you carry the news instead. A Cleaner has his name, his
pin, and his logs, and chose not to use them yet. That buys my squad the
only armour that fits us. Being more trouble dead than alive. He'll
understand that arithmetic. It's the only kind he does.\"

For the first time, something moved behind the man's eyes that wasn't
warmth or its imitation.

\"The file said filler,\" he said.

\"It's a good file,\" Spauld said. \"Mind the fault. Eleven minutes.\"

The man considered the empty place on his collar where the pin had been,
and inclined his head, unhurried to the last, and stepped back into the
corridor. Spauld listened to him go. Footsteps, then the dolly wheel's
squeak, then orders given too low to carve words from, and then only the
plant breathing and the core ticking its last entries into his pocket.

Eleven minutes, he'd said. He checked the sidearm out of habit rather
than need, and got to work.

### 3

The chemics took six. He worked fast and clean along the feed junctions,
scorch gel where the ether lines crossed the climate plant, a timing
vial cracked and wedged where the heat would find it, and his hands did
the work while his head sat quietly with what he'd just done. He'd let
the recruiter go. He'd traded justice for insurance, and he'd done it
in a room where a farmer had died wearing restraint straps, and the
farmer would have understood the trade and hated it, and both of those
things could be true, and that was the north for you.

He was topside with three minutes to spare. The squad lay along the
ridge, rifles out, and far across the basin a line of shapes was filing
away east with crates between them, unhurried, already erasing
themselves into the dark.

\"That them?\" Moll said.

\"That's them.\"

\"And you let them go.\"

\"I let them carry a message.\"

\"A message.\" She kept her glass on the distant line of them. \"Who
reads it?\"

\"Somebody south.\"

\"That's not an answer.\"

\"It's the one I've got tonight.\" He heard how it sounded. He said it
anyway.

Moll lowered the glass, and there was the flat calm on her that he'd
last seen down a sidearm's length in a cold room. \"You unsealed your
mask for a dying stranger. The whole squad's carrying your lie south,
Earl's carrying the order for it, and the kid's carrying what he saw
on that table. And you're up here handing out messages you won't read
to us.\" She wasn't angry. That was the worst of it. She was doing
arithmetic. \"The seal's still broken, Spauld. I'm waiting to see what
else gets in through it.\"

He had three answers and kept all of them.

The ground finished the conversation for them. A deep cough under the
basin floor, felt in the teeth more than heard, then a second, and the
arc of tagged trees exhaled a ring of dust, and somewhere below, the
machine that had breathed for that place stopped breathing. No gout of
flame. No spectacle. Just a hum that had been in the ground all day,
gone, and its absence louder than the blast.

Petey let out a long breath. \"That's it? Kind of quiet.\"

\"Good demolition's like good surgery,\" Moll said. \"If it's loud,
somebody made a mistake.\"

Gord said nothing at all. He was looking east, where the shapes had
gone, with an expression Spauld didn't try to read.

### 4

They broke camp in the last of the dark and made the Garrote's mouth at
first grey, and the Garrote had changed overnight.

Spauld smelled it before the light showed it. Green, wet, sweetish. The
smell of a burn before there was a burn. Then the channel turned and
there it was, growth across the first pinch point, wall to wall and
chest high. Young Sprawl, pale, colourless at the tips, grown in the
dark of a single night. And moving. A slow restlessness that did not
stop when they came near.

That was the first thing Spauld marked. It didn't go still.

\"That weren't here,\" Petey breathed. \"We walked this yesterday.
Nothin' grows that fast.\"

\"Engineered does.\" Spauld crouched at the edge of it. The growth came
up in spaced clumps, regular, a sown line rather than a spread, and
half-buried under the nearest clump lay the burst casing of something
fist-sized, waxed canvas and wire. He didn't touch it. \"Seedbombs.
They sowed the channel behind them on the way out.\"

\"Closing the road,\" Earl said.

\"Closing the road.\" And telling whoever came next what kind of men had
gone before, though Spauld kept that part in his pocket with the rest of
his messages.

There was no going around. That was the Garrote's whole argument. There
was the channel, or there was a week.

\"Burner forward,\" Earl said. \"Fighting burn, by the book, and the
book says slow.\" He looked at Gord's dressed arm, at the rig, at the
wind funnelling straight up the cut into their faces. \"Wind's wrong.\"

\"Wind's always wrong,\" Gord said, and shouldered the pack.

A fighting burn went nothing like a pocket. Moll wet the walls upslope
as far as the sprayer would throw. Petey rode the rig from behind his
uncle, calling pressure by feel, one hand flat on the tank the way
you'd take a pulse. Gord put the flame in low and walked it forward a
stride at a time, and the growth did what young growth did, hissed and
crisped and fell back, and did the other thing too, the thing that made
the north the north. It leaned in. Toward the heat, toward the movement.
Runners felt along the scorched stone at the edges of the flame like
fingers reading, and everywhere the fire wasn't, it thickened.

\"It's comin' in behind the burn line,\" Petey called. \"Uncle, wider
on the fan.\"

\"Wider spends faster.\"

\"Then spend it. It's flankin' us.\"

Halfway up the pinch, with black behind them and green ahead and the
wind throwing the heat back over the squad in waves, the rig's temper
arrived. The note of the flame climbed, guttered, climbed again.
Petey's voice went flat the way Moll's did in the worst of it.
\"Valve's walkin'. Shut it down or it decides for us.\"

\"Shut it and we're standing in a hallway it's growing closed,\" Earl
said.

\"Then I fix it hot.\" The kid was already into the housing. \"Thirty
counts. Hold the line.\"

Thirty counts, four Cleaners, and a channel of moving green. Moll
emptied the sprayer's last pressure into the leading edge. Spauld
unhooked his lantern and held it out at arm's length ahead, because the
green stopped for lantern light the way it stopped for nothing else, an
old trick that worked for reasons no pamphlet had ever offered, and the
runners nearest the glow slowed, hesitated, argued with themselves. And
Gord, with the flame shut and the housing ticking with heat, laid his
bare forearm, the burned one, along the top of the tank to hold the
assembly steady against the wind while his nephew's fingers worked
inside it.

\"Say when,\" he said, and nothing else, and if it cost him what it had
to be costing him, the only sign was the sweat cutting lines through the
soot on his face.

\"When,\" Petey said.

The flame came back full and clean, and Gord walked it up the rest of
the pinch without a word, and the channel opened ahead of them one
burned stride at a time, and then they were through, into moving air and
open rock, coughing, scorched, alive.

They stood a minute. Nobody called it a rest.

Petey sat down where he'd stopped, hands shaking now that they were
allowed to, and watched them shake with a kind of distant interest. Moll
crouched in front of him and ran the medic's circuit, mask seal,
pupils, the backs of his hands.

\"You'll do,\" she said, and this time Spauld heard the whole sentence
underneath it.

Gord let her dress the arm again without being chased about it, which
told its own story.

---

They walked the rest of it in the grey, boots in warm ash for the first
hundred strides, and Earl dropped back beside Spauld where the channel
widened enough for two. For a while he didn't speak. The wind did its
work on them.

\"The logs,\" Earl said finally.

\"In my coat. With the note from the console.\"

\"And down there. Before the fault.\" Earl's eyes stayed forward.
\"Anyone?\"

The question hung there, and Spauld understood exactly what it was,
because he'd watched Earl swallow the same shape of question at
Ironmarch, at the residue circles, at the hatch. Here was the place to
lay it all down. The pin in his pocket. Halvery's name. The offer, and
the trade, and the message walking east.

\"No one,\" Spauld said. \"Place was empty.\"

Earl walked on a dozen paces. Old boots on frost-cracked stone.

\"Empty,\" he said. \"That's how it'll read, then.\"

He knew. Spauld heard it plain, and heard underneath it the thing that
had made Earl what he was for thirty years, the terrible practical mercy
of a man who has learned which truths keep his people alive and which
ones get squads sent north into a report that's already written. Earl
had spent a career being told no one, and saying it forward, and hating
it, and here he was on the receiving end at last, taking it like
medicine from a man he'd trained without meaning to.

\"For what it's worth,\" Earl said, \"when you've thought on it. I'd
like to be told before it happens. Not after. I've had a lifetime of
after.\"

\"You will.\"

Earl nodded once and lengthened his stride, and that was the whole of
it, and it was more than Spauld had ever heard him say about anything.

### 5

Fort Ironmarch received them the way it had sent them off, which is to
say it barely noticed. The same guards, or two just like them. The
graffiti over the command door hadn't been cleaned. THE BLEEDIN'
NORTH. Somebody's truth, left standing because taking it down was work.

Moll sat at the comms panel and keyed in the squad code without
ceremony. Earl stood at her shoulder, and before she spoke he looked
once across the room at Spauld. Not a question. A door held open a
moment, in case. Spauld gave nothing back, and Earl turned to the panel.

\"Squad Iron-Three reporting. Outpost swept and logged cold. Site clear.
No survivors. Returning south.\"

Earl's words. Her voice, level all the way through.

Static took it. Somewhere a relay wrote it down, and no one at the other
end had to be listening, and the record would show everything had worked
exactly as intended. Which, Spauld reflected, for once it had. Just not
theirs.

She sat back and looked at her hands. \"I became a Cleaner because it
was the lesser evil,\" she said, to nobody in particular. \"Be nice to
know the exchange rate's still holding.\"

\"It ain't a rate,\" Gord said from the doorway. \"It's a toll. You
pay it every trip, and it goes up.\" He shouldered the incinerator pack,
shimmed valve and all. \"You still crossin', though. That's the part
they don't put on the sign.\"

Petey looked between the two of them, and didn't ask a question, and
Spauld marked the date. The kid had come north with a light in his gut
and he still had it, but it was banked now, shielded, a working flame
instead of a bonfire. That was either growing up or the first instalment
of the toll. Probably there was no way to tell the difference.

### 6

The train came in the afternoon, and it left the way trains left, steady
and certain and indifferent to its cargo. They took the same cabin. Gord
was asleep before Northrail, chin on his chest plate, and this time
Petey sat with his uncle's incinerator pack between his knees, running
the pressure lines through his fingers like a man reading. Moll checked
her medkit straps. They were fine. She checked them anyway.

Spauld sat by the window with the note and the log core an inch from his
heart and a gold wheat sheaf in his pocket, small enough to miss if you
weren't hungry, and watched the Garrote country slide away behind them.

He didn't know yet what he'd do with any of it. Halvery would hear his
message inside a week. The cohort was out there in its clusters,
dreaming each other, waiting to be spent by one owner or another. And
somewhere under all of it, patient as arithmetic, the green kept its own
ledger.

The train slowed for the grade south of the fort, and that was when he
saw it.

The hare sat between the rails ahead, in the middle of the cleared
right-of-way where no hare had any business being, fur grey-green in
patches, eyes holding the light. It didn't startle at the engine. It
turned, unhurried, and walked south along the track bed in a perfectly
straight line, keeping just ahead of them, following the iron road down
toward the belt, toward Ashbarrow, toward everywhere the trains went.

Spauld watched it until the curve took it from view. Then he leaned his
head against the cool glass, and closed his eyes, and did not sleep.
